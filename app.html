Critical periods

Runs of unusual burning

Each day's 7-day average of harmonised activity is compared with the same week in every other year. It is flagged at twice the usual level (and 2 robust deviations), or 3 robust deviations (and 1.5×), when the extra burning is also large for this area. Three or more flagged days in a row (one quiet day allowed) form a period.

Year by year

The instrument steps, corrected

Each new satellite adds more looks at the same fires, and VIIRS's 375 m pixels find many small fires that MODIS's 1 km pixels miss. Summed as detected, the record jumps when Aqua (2002) and VIIRS (2012) arrive. The harmonised record should not. Harmonised bars sit lower because each satellite is scaled to the reference sensor and the satellites are averaged rather than added, so a fire seen five times counts once.

Around the world

Five fire regions, one method

Our home state, Texas, beside four regions with Mediterranean climates: California, Chile, Portugal and Greece. Harmonised the same way, their records can be compared directly. Texas burns most in late winter and early spring, when dry grass and strong winds meet; Chile's season is the mirror image of California's, because its summer is our winter.

Does harmonisation work?

Sensor calibration and validation

Ratio = the sensor's all-year total ÷ the reference's, on days both observed this area (hotspots: all detections; fire power: fires above the floor). The factor applied is its inverse, set separately for each calendar month; the column shows the smallest and largest. Daily spread = half the interquartile range of the daily ratios. Held-out check: fit on even years only, then compare each sensor's odd-year monthly totals with the reference's, before and after correction.

Method

How the record is built

  1. Clean. Find satellite outages and exclude those days for that satellite, and flag fixed industrial heat sources in recent near-real-time rows.
  2. Filter. Keep detections inside the area, at or above the chosen confidence, and (optionally) drop static and offshore sources such as gas flares, using FIRMS's type field.
  3. Aggregate. Sum fire radiative power and count detections per sensor, per day, per day or night pass. A second sum counts only "common" fires above the floor.
  4. Calibrate. Put every sensor on the scale of one reference (MODIS Aqua by default, an afternoon sensor with the longest record), separately for day and night passes, from the ratio of totals on days both observed the area, with a separate factor for each calendar month (on California data the VIIRS-to-MODIS ratio ranges from about 4× in winter to almost 6× in spring). A month or area with few detections leans on the all-year and whole-dataset factors. Hotspot counts use all detections, because VIIRS's 375 m pixels report several times more hotspots than MODIS's 1 km pixels. Fire power uses only fires above the floor, which both instruments can see. This also corrects the Terra-only years before Aqua launched in 2002.
  5. Combine. A day's harmonised value is the average of the calibrated sensors that were in orbit, so one fire seen by five satellites counts once. A sensor in orbit that saw nothing counts as zero.
  6. Summarise. Monthly totals are compared with the median and the average of the same month in every other complete year. The latest month is compared day for day with the same dates in other years.
  7. Score. Each day's trailing 7-day average is compared with the median of the same seven-day window in every other year (its own season). The ratio, a robust deviation (interquartile range ÷ 1.349) and the size of the extra burning decide whether it is flagged. At least three years are needed.

Limitations

  • A detection is a hot pixel, not a confirmed wildfire, and its location is the pixel centre.
  • Clouds and smoke hide fires. FIRMS hotspot files carry no cloud mask, so some quiet days are hidden days.
  • Fire power is a snapshot at overpass time, not a measure of burned area or emissions.
  • MODIS overpass times have drifted in recent years, so the reference itself is not perfectly stable.
  • Satellite outages are found automatically (a satellite reports nothing while its partner says it should have seen at least 20 hotspots) and excluded, so they don't read as quiet days. A short outage in a quiet season can still slip through.
  • Near-real-time rows have no source type, so fixed industrial heat sources are flagged by matching ~1 km cells that the archive marks as static.
  • This harmonisation is the team's own estimate, not an official NASA product.
