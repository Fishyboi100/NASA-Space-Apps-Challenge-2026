Harmonising MODIS and VIIRS active-fire hotspots into one consistent record

Ember Sync methods · NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026: Harmonization of MODIS and VIIRS Hot Spots · Nish Gokhale and Amogh Kashyap, Sugar Land, Texas

In one minute

NASA's fire record comes from five satellites that joined at different times. Each new one adds more detections, so the record jumps even when the fires don't. Ember Sync puts every satellite on the same scale as MODIS Aqua, so 2005 and 2025 can be compared fairly.

  • +430% → −1% the jump when VIIRS arrived in 2012, before and after harmonising (California)
  • 0.67 → 0.96 match with CAL FIRE's official acres burned, 2003–2025
  • ±4% how far the five satellites disagree after calibration, in a typical year
  • 5,108,993 NASA detections, each checked against the original FIRMS files

The problem

MODIS (Terra 2000, Aqua 2002) sees fire in 1 km pixels. VIIRS (S-NPP 2012, NOAA-20 2018, NOAA-21 2023) sees 375 m pixels and finds many more small fires. Added together as detected, California's yearly hotspot count climbs to 24 times what one steady satellite reports, purely because satellites were added.

Our method

  1. Clean. Find days when a satellite was offline (its partner satellites saw fire, it saw none) and leave them out. Remove gas flares and other static heat sources.
  2. Calibrate. On days both observed the area, compare each satellite's total with MODIS Aqua's, separately for day and night and for each month. Months with little data borrow from the satellite's yearly factor.
  3. Combine. Scale each satellite to Aqua's level and average the satellites that observed each day, so a fire seen five times counts once.
  4. Compare. Flag days, months and seasons that are far above what's normal for that place and time of year.
Hd = mean over observing satellites of Ns,d ⁄ fs,day/night,month

Without being told where to look, it ranks the Smokehouse Creek Fire (February 2024, 68 times a usual February) first in the Texas Panhandle, and finds the August Complex, the Camp Fire, Chile's January 2017 and Portugal's June 2017 as record months.

Limitations

  • Hotspots measure fire seen at overpass time, not burned area. Clouds and smoke can hide fires, and farm and prescribed burning is counted.
  • One factor per month can leave a small step where the mix of fire sizes changes a lot (Texas statewide, +16%). Calibrating by land cover is our next step.
  • This is our own estimate, not an official NASA product.

Cleaning

Satellite outages

A day with no detections can mean no fire or a satellite that was not working. For each satellite we predict its expected count from partner satellites observing the same area (Terra and Aqua for each other; for each VIIRS satellite, the other VIIRS satellites, then Aqua). A run of zero days is marked as an outage when the partners imply at least 20 expected detections over two or more days, or at least 40 in total. Outage days are excluded from that satellite's coverage, so they never read as quiet fire days. In California this found, for example, Aqua offline from 31 March to 19 April 2022 and S-NPP from 27 July to 10 August 2022.

Static heat sources

Gas flares, refineries and other persistent industrial sources are not wildfires. Archive detections flagged by FIRMS as type 1 to 3 (volcanoes, other static land sources, offshore) are excluded by default. Near-real-time rows have no type, so they are excluded when they fall in a 0.01° neighbourhood (3 × 3 cells) where the archive has at least three static detections.

Confidence

By default we keep nominal and high-confidence detections (MODIS confidence 30 or above; VIIRS "n" and "h"). Users can include all detections or only high confidence.

Calibration

MODIS Aqua is the reference: it has the longest uninterrupted record, overlaps every VIIRS satellite, and its afternoon overpass is close to the VIIRS overpasses. For each other satellite s, pass p (day or night) and calendar month m, we compare totals over the days when both s and Aqua observed the area:

fs,p,m = Σshared days Ns ⁄ Σshared days NAqua

A ratio of totals is robust to the many days with zero or one detection, where daily ratios are undefined or extreme. Months with little shared fire are pulled towards broader estimates by shrinkage, first towards the satellite's all-month factor for that pass, then towards the whole-region factor:

f′s,p,m = ( n · fs,p,m + 200 · fprior ) ⁄ ( n + 200 )

where n is the number of shared detections. We tested three ways to calibrate (one factor per satellite, one per season, one per month) by fitting on even years and testing on odd years. Per-month factors were best (median monthly error 8.3%, against 11.0% for a single factor), because the ratio between instruments changes through the year: in California, VIIRS sees 4.1 to 5.8 times more daytime hotspots than Aqua depending on the month, and far more at night.

Hotspot counts are calibrated using all detections. Fire radiative power is calibrated only on detections of at least 10 MW, which both instruments detect reliably, so VIIRS's many small fires do not distort the power ratio.

The harmonised record

Each satellite's daily count in the chosen area is divided by its factor, giving an Aqua-equivalent estimate. The harmonised value for the day is the mean over the satellites that observed it (not in outage):

Hd = (1 ⁄ |Sd|) · Σs ∈ Sd Ns,d ⁄ f′s,p,m(d)

Averaging rather than summing means a fire seen by five satellites counts once, and adding or losing a satellite changes the precision, not the level. Days with no observing satellite are left blank rather than set to zero.

Finding the unusual

Days and critical periods

For each day we take the trailing 7-day mean of the harmonised record and compare it with the same ±3-day window in every other year (at least three years). The baseline is the median of those years and the spread is a robust standard deviation, the interquartile range divided by 1.349. A day is flagged when it is at least twice the baseline and 2 robust deviations above it, or 3 robust deviations and 1.5 times above it, and the extra burning is at least 15% of the area's 95th-percentile day. Three or more flagged days in a row (one quiet day allowed) form a critical period; periods are ranked by total extra burning.

Monthly calendar

Each month is compared with the median and average of the same calendar month in other years. A month is outlined when it is at least twice its median, the extra burning is at least 10% of the area's busiest typical month, and, for hotspot counts, the excess is larger than chance: at least 3√max(median, 1) + 2.

Early warning: the season so far

The fire year starts in the area's quietest calendar month (lowest median), so a fire season is never split in two and the Southern Hemisphere needs no special case. For the current fire year we compute the running total to the latest day of data and compare it with every complete past fire year at the same date, giving the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles and a rank. The season is "above normal" at or above the 75th percentile and "well above normal" at or above the 90th percentile and at least 1.5 times the median. In California the 2026 season was 59% below the median on 14 November, matching CAL FIRE's own year-to-date figure (339,451 acres against a five-year average of 1,101,011).

Validation

The instrument step

If harmonisation works, the record should not jump when a satellite joins. To separate the satellite effect from real changes in fire, we divide each yearly total by MODIS Aqua's own total for that year and compare the three years after VIIRS S-NPP arrived (2012–2014) with the three years before (2009–2011).

Held-out years

Fitting the factors on even years and testing on odd years (months with at least 30 reference detections), VIIRS S-NPP's monthly error against MODIS Aqua falls from 618% as detected to 13% harmonised.

Known events

Without being told where to look, the critical-period ranking finds documented fires: the August Complex (September 2020, 19.8 times a usual September statewide), the Camp Fire (November 2018, 31 times a usual November around Paradise), the June 2008 lightning siege, the Smokehouse Creek Fire (25 February to 8 March 2024, first in the Texas Panhandle, 68 times a usual February) and the East Amarillo Complex (March 2006, second in the Panhandle).

Data integrity

Every detection in the original FIRMS files was compared with the app's packed data: date, time, satellite, day or night, confidence class and type match exactly for all 5,108,993 records, positions agree within the packing precision (under 35 m) and fire power within 0.01%.

Uncertainty

We report how far the satellites disagree after calibration. For each period, each satellite's calibrated total is computed on its own (scaled to the days the harmonised record covers, for satellites that observed at least 90% of them). The lowest and highest of these form the range shown as whiskers on yearly totals, in the monthly calendar and in every search. In California a typical year's range is within ±4% of the harmonised value. Before July 2002 only Terra observed, so those years have no cross-check and are shown without a range.

Reproducibility

Everything runs in the browser from the FIRMS files; the same code produces the app's views and the published harmonised datasets (daily 0.1° grid, regional daily and monthly series, calibration factors and outages for all five regions). The grid sums match the app's regional record to within 0.05 hotspots per day. Scripts to pack the data, build the datasets and verify every record against the FIRMS files are published with the code: github.com/Fishyboi100/NASA-Space-Apps-Challenge-2026.
