Our home state

Was the largest fire in Texas history unusual?

In February 2024 the Smokehouse Creek Fire burned 1,058,482 acres of the Texas Panhandle. We're from Sugar Land, Texas, and we wanted to know how it compared with the past. The satellite record couldn't say. So we built Ember Sync, drew a box around the Panhandle, and told it nothing else.

Seen from space

The satellites saw the scars. Ember Sync found the fires.

Days after the fires, NASA's Terra and Landsat 8 satellites photographed the burned land: a scar more than 100 km long across the Panhandle, and charred grazing land around Fritch and Lake Meredith.

Ember Sync works from a different NASA record: the hotspots the satellites detected while the fires burned. Without being shown these pictures, it put the same place and the same days at the top of 26 years of history.

Every NASA fire detection in the Texas Panhandle, 2000 to 2026, in Ember Sync. The long streaks are wind-driven grass fires.

The problem

Why the raw record can't be trusted

The record is built by a changing team of satellites. Each new one sees more fire than the last, so the raw counts tell a story about satellites, not fire.

More satellites, more looks

Five satellites now pass over every day, against one in 2001. The same fire gets counted again on every pass.

Sharper eyes

A VIIRS pixel covers 7 times less ground than a MODIS pixel, so it sees small fires MODIS misses: 4 to 6 times more by day.

Gaps that look like calm

When a satellite goes offline for days, the record dips. Without a check, a broken satellite reads as a quiet fire week.

How it works

Three steps to a fair record

Run in your browser on the original NASA FIRMS detections, for whatever area and dates you choose.

All 2.2 million NASA fire detections in California, 2000 to 2026, before harmonisation.

  1. Clean

    Find the days each satellite was offline by checking its partner satellites, and leave them out, so a broken satellite never reads as a quiet fire week. Remove gas flares, factories and other heat sources that aren't wildfires.

  2. Calibrate

    On days when two satellites both saw the area, learn how many hotspots each reports compared with MODIS Aqua. Separately for day and night passes and for each month, because the ratio changes with the season (4 to 6 times by day for VIIRS).

  3. Combine

    Scale every satellite to Aqua's level and average the ones that observed each day. A fire seen five times counts once, and the record means the same in 2005 as in 2025.

The science

How we know it works

We tested the harmonised record against data it was never fitted to: official burned area, years held back from the fit, and the original NASA files. And we show its uncertainty instead of hiding it.

The calibration, in two lines

For each satellite, day or night pass and calendar month, compare totals on the days both it and MODIS Aqua observed the area:

fs,p,m = Σ Ns ⁄ Σ NAqua
f′ = (n·f + 200·fprior) ⁄ (n + 200)

Months with few shared detections lean on the whole-year and whole-region factors. Each day's harmonised value is the average over the satellites that observed it:

Hday = means observing ( Ns,day ⁄ f′s,p,m )

Fire power is calibrated the same way, using only fires above 10 MW, which both kinds of satellite detect.

Early warning

Is this season running hot?

Ember Sync compares this fire season's running total with every past year at the same date, and labels it below normal, normal, above normal or well above normal. The fire year starts in each place's quietest month, so the Southern Hemisphere works too.

Right now California's 2026 season is running 59% below a typical year. CAL FIRE's own count agrees: 339,451 acres so far against a five-year average of 1,101,011.

Conclusion

What we found

We set out to answer one question about our home state. Along the way we built a fair fire record for anywhere on Earth.

Yes, it was unusual

The Smokehouse Creek Fire was the most unusual burning in the Texas Panhandle in 26 years of satellite records: February 2024 had 68 times a usual February, ahead of the 2006 East Amarillo Complex.

The record can be fixed

Calibrating each satellite to MODIS Aqua removes the jumps (+430% to −1% in California), matches official burned area (0.96) and keeps the satellites within ±4% of each other.

It works anywhere

The same method, unchanged, finds the worst months on record in five regions on three continents, from California's August Complex to Chile's January 2017.

Why it matters

MODIS is near the end of its life, and VIIRS will carry the fire record on alone. Without harmonisation, every comparison across that handover is distorted. With it, a fire manager, a scientist or a student can ask whether a fire or a season is truly unusual for that place, and get an answer they can check, download and share.

What comes next

  • Calibrate by land cover, so grass fires, forest fires and farm fires each get their own factor
  • Use cloud cover, so days when fires were hidden aren't read as quiet days

About us

Who we are

We are Ember Sync, a team of students from Sugar Land, Texas, taking part in the 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge.

We chose the MODIS and VIIRS challenge because of a question close to home. In February 2024 the Smokehouse Creek Fire became the largest wildfire in Texas history. Was it really as unusual as it felt? Answering that honestly meant fixing the satellite record first, and the fix turned out to work far beyond Texas.

How we built it

All of the data comes from NASA FIRMS: 5,108,993 fire detections from five satellites, downloaded as archive and near-real-time files for five regions. We processed them in Python, checked every region's detection count against the original NASA files, and compared our results with CAL FIRE's official burned-area records.

The app is plain HTML and JavaScript. Each region's 26-year record is compressed into the page, so it runs in any browser, on a phone or a laptop, with no login, no server and nothing to install. We tested it automatically on desktop and phone screens, in all four languages.

Questions

Where does the data come from?

NASA's Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS): MODIS Collection 6.1 from Terra and Aqua, and VIIRS 375 m Collection 2 from S-NPP, NOAA-20 and NOAA-21. Five regions are built in, from November 2000 to November 2026. For anywhere else, download the free files from FIRMS and load them in the app.

Why MODIS Aqua as the reference?

It has watched the longest without a break (since July 2002), it overlaps every VIIRS satellite, and its afternoon pass is close to the VIIRS passes, so they see fires at a similar time of day.

Why are harmonised numbers smaller than raw ones?

Each fire is counted once, at MODIS Aqua's level, instead of once per satellite with VIIRS's extra sensitivity. Harmonised numbers are for comparing places and years with each other.

What can't it tell you?

Hotspots are fires seen when a satellite passed, not the area burned. Clouds and smoke hide some fires, farm burning is counted, and before July 2002 only one satellite watched. The early warning compares with history; it is not a forecast. The harmonisation is our own estimate, not an official NASA product.

Why does Texas keep a +16% jump?

Its record fire year, 2011, came just before VIIRS arrived, and big wildfires and small farm fires don't scale between the two kinds of satellite in quite the same way. In the Panhandle alone the jump is +1%. Calibrating by land cover is our next step to close the gap.

Can I use the data?

Yes. Every view downloads as CSV, and the harmonised datasets for all five regions, with their calibration factors and satellite outages, are free to use.
